Key terms used in this proposal

Interoperability
Chargers, networks, vehicles and payment systems from different companies working together through common standards.
CPO
Charge Point Operator. A company that operates charging stations and the software that manages them.
eMSP
e-Mobility Service Provider. A company that gives drivers access to charging through an app, card or account.
CPMS
Charge Point Management System. The operator’s back office for chargers, sessions and tariffs.
OCPP
Open Charge Point Protocol. Open standard for communication between a charger and its management system.
OCPI
Open Charge Point Interface. Open protocol for sharing locations, tariffs, sessions and billing records between networks.
ISO 15118
International standard for communication between the electric vehicle and the charger.
Roaming
Charging on another operator’s network using the account of your own service provider.
CDR
Charge Detail Record. The standard record of a completed charging session, used for billing.
Plug & Charge
The vehicle identifies itself when plugged in, so charging and billing start automatically.
Smart charging
Adjusting when and how fast vehicles charge in response to grid conditions, prices or demand.
V2G
Vehicle-to-grid. Using electric vehicle batteries to supply electricity back to the grid.

Part I

Context

Why interoperability matters, and why Nigeria should act while its electric mobility market is still young.

Executive summary1. Introduction2. The opportunity before Nigeria3. The problem4. The consequences of fragmentation

Nigeria is at an important stage in the development of its electric mobility industry.

Activity is increasing across

Electric vehiclesCharging infrastructureBattery technologyRenewable energyFleet electrificationElectric busesElectric motorcyclesDigital platforms

A growing number of Nigerian and international companies are entering different parts of the emerging ecosystem.

The opportunity

This growth presents Nigeria with a significant opportunity to shape how the industry develops.

The risk

Without a common national framework, charging networks, vehicle manufacturers, station operators, payment systems, mobile applications and software platforms could each operate independently of one another.

Fragmentation would

  • Increase the cost of developing the industry
  • Create unnecessary barriers for consumers
  • Discourage international investment
  • Increase technology risk
  • Leave proprietary ecosystems that are difficult and expensive to connect later

The Government has an opportunity to act before the market becomes fragmented.

Government sets the standards and rules. The private sector builds and operates the market.

The objective is not for Government to own or operate the country’s charging networks, but to allow privately operated networks to work together.

SON has already recognised the importance of safety, reliability and interoperability in Nigeria’s electric mobility ecosystem and has initiated stakeholder engagement on EV standardisation. This proposal builds on that direction and translates it into a coordinated national implementation framework.

Electric mobility is no longer simply an automotive issue.

Success requires more than increasing the number of electric vehicles or charging stations. Nigeria needs an ecosystem in which these components communicate and operate together.

That is the fundamental purpose of interoperability

  • A vehicle can access a charging station regardless of the company that owns it.
  • A charging-network operator can connect with other networks.
  • A mobility service provider can offer its customers access to multiple charging networks.
  • A charger manufacturer can supply hardware to different CPOs without unnecessary proprietary dependencies.
  • An investor can enter the Nigerian market without recreating the entire digital and regulatory infrastructure.

Nigeria can establish common rules before incompatible systems become deeply embedded.

The Nigerian market is still relatively young, an advantage many mature EV markets did not have when their charging ecosystems began developing.

The preferable path

Establish the rules now

  • Government sets common rules while the market is still young
  • Private-sector competition takes place within an open framework
  • Chargers, platforms and contracts are built on shared standards from the start

The path to avoid

Fix fragmentation later

  • Thousands of chargers, vehicles, software platforms, payment systems and contracts are built around proprietary technologies
  • Correcting fragmentation becomes expensive and politically difficult
  • Interoperability problems must be resolved several years later

Nigeria has a window of opportunity: establish the rules now, then let competition happen within an open framework.

The framework

Government does not need to own Nigeria’s charging infrastructure. Its role is to create an environment in which private capital can invest with confidence. The framework rests on six principles.

Consumer choice

Safety and reliability

Interoperability must not compromise electrical safety or service quality.

Investment readiness

The interface

The Open Charge Point Protocol should be a principal standard for communication between the charge point and the charging management system.

Why it matters

A CPO should be able to change its charging management platform without necessarily replacing all of its physical chargers.

How to adopt it

Government should undertake a formal technical assessment and adopt appropriate versions of OCPP, alongside OCPI, ISO 15118 and recognised safety, metering and cybersecurity standards.

OCPI enables national roaming, so one account can charge across many networks.

OCPI, or an equivalent internationally recognised roaming standard, should form part of Nigeria’s interoperability framework.

Charge anywhere in Nigeria

A phased roadmap toward ISO 15118, Plug & Charge and vehicle-to-grid.

ISO 15118 provides the foundation for a progression of future capabilities.

  1. Vehicle-to-charger communication

    The vehicle and charger exchange identity and charging information.

  2. Automated authentication

    The vehicle identifies itself securely, without cards or apps.

  3. Plug & Charge

    Plug in, and charging and billing begin automatically.

  4. Smart charging

    Charging adapts to grid conditions, prices and demand.

  5. Energy management

    Chargers, storage and buildings coordinate their energy use.

  6. Vehicle-to-grid, eventually

    Vehicle batteries can supply electricity back to the grid.

Not everything at once

Nigeria should not necessarily mandate every element of ISO 15118 immediately.

Future-proof what is built today

A phased adoption roadmap ensures charging infrastructure is not built in a way that prevents these technologies from being introduced later.

Five platform phases, from national standards to national interoperability.

    Enable roaming, national charging discovery, cross-network access and comprehensive e-mobility reporting.

Ten initial actions recommended for the Honourable Minister’s approval.

  1. Direct the relevant Ministry and agencies to establish a working group on EV charging interoperability.
  2. Commission a national assessment of existing EV charging infrastructure, software platforms and technical standards.
  3. Develop a draft National EV Charging Interoperability Standard.
  4. Develop the National EV Charging Station Registry specification.
  5. Begin industry consultation with CPOs, eMSPs, charger and vehicle manufacturers, and technology providers.
  6. Establish an EV interoperability testing and certification programme.
  7. Develop a national charging-roaming framework.
  8. Make interoperability a requirement for relevant government-funded EV infrastructure.

  10. Position Nigeria to lead the development of an African and ECOWAS EV interoperability framework.

Proposed national policy statement, for consideration

“The Federal Republic of Nigeria shall promote an open, safe, reliable, competitive and interoperable electric mobility ecosystem in which publicly accessible electric vehicle charging infrastructure is capable of communicating across approved technical platforms and networks, enabling consumers, businesses and mobility service providers to access charging services without unnecessary technological barriers.”

Build the market, not the monopoly.

The Nigerian EV industry should not be built around

  • One company
  • One charging network
  • One application
  • One technology provider

It should be built around common rules that allow many companies to compete.

Government establishes

  • Standards
  • Safety
  • Regulation
  • Certification
  • Data
  • Interoperability
  • Consumer protection
  • Investment framework

The private sector builds

  • Chargers
  • Networks
  • Software
  • Mobility services
  • Energy infrastructure
  • Payment solutions
  • Fleet solutions
  • Innovation

This separation of responsibilities lets Nigeria benefit from private-sector innovation while maintaining national coordination.

The market is still young, so decisions made today will shape the industry for decades.

The Government does not need to

  • Predict which charging company will become the largest
  • Select a single technology provider
  • Build every charging station

Instead, it can create an environment in which the best companies compete and the best technologies succeed. Interoperability should be treated as national digital and energy infrastructure, not as a technical feature of individual charging companies.

If Nigeria establishes this principle early, it can avoid many of the fragmentation problems experienced by emerging infrastructure markets, and position itself as one of Africa’s leading destinations for electric mobility investment.

The objective

“A customer should be able to drive an electric vehicle anywhere in Nigeria and charge it wherever an authorised charging station is available, regardless of which company owns the station or which mobility service the customer uses.”

The opportunity is much larger than charging stations: an interoperable national ecosystem supporting private investment, local innovation, international participation and eventual regional integration.

It is respectfully recommended that the Federal Ministry of Power consider initiating the development of a National Electric Vehicle Charging Interoperability Framework.
