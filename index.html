Part of the National EV Charging Interoperability Framework

Lean MVP / National Interoperability Pilot

Start with a controlled national pilot. Prove interoperability before scaling nationally.

Nigeria does not need to build the complete national e-mobility infrastructure on day one. Government can instead establish a controlled interoperability environment involving a small number of charging operators, mobility service providers, charger manufacturers, charging stations, payment providers, technology providers and relevant public agencies.

Within that environment, the pilot demonstrates a single proposition: that independently operated charging infrastructure and digital platforms can communicate using common standards, under a national registry and monitoring layer.

The Lean MVP is the first executable step toward the full National Electric Vehicle Charging Interoperability Framework. It does not replace the national framework, the National E-Mobility Platform or the five-year roadmap set out in the preceding sections. It accelerates them.

Why a controlled pilot comes before national deployment

The full national programme set out in this proposal spans eighteen interdependent workstreams. Each is necessary. Building all of them at once is not.

  • Policy
  • Governance
  • Standards
  • Certification
  • Registry
  • Cybersecurity
  • Data infrastructure
  • Interoperability
  • Roaming
  • Compliance
  • Reliability monitoring
  • Grid integration
  • Consumer protection
  • National reporting
  • ISO 15118 and Plug & Charge
  • Smart charging
  • Regional interoperability
  • Enforcement

Delivering these simultaneously would commit public funds and institutional capacity to a design that has not yet been tested against real operators and real hardware. It introduces avoidable cost, complexity and implementation risk, and it defers the moment at which government learns whether the technical model works.

The Lean MVP therefore narrows the first phase to one question.

Can independently operated charging networks in Nigeria communicate through common standards, and give government a trusted view of charging infrastructure and interoperability?

Everything the pilot builds exists to answer that question. Everything else is deferred, not abandoned.

Pilot objective

Prove interoperability end to end

Eleven conditions define the technical demonstration. The pilot succeeds when all eleven can be observed in sequence, across systems operated by different organisations.

  1. A charger connects to a CPO platform.
  2. The charger communicates using OCPP.
  3. The CPO exposes charging information through OCPI.
  4. An eMSP discovers the charging location.
  5. The eMSP initiates an authorised charging workflow.
  6. Charging session information is exchanged between systems.
  7. A Charge Detail Record is generated.
  8. Payment and reconciliation information can be represented.
  9. Charger availability and faults are visible.
  10. The national platform receives standardised monitoring data.
  11. The system can identify whether participating infrastructure meets the defined interoperability requirements.

The test that decides the pilot

One charging session, across two independent companies

CPO A owns the charger. eMSP B has the customer. Neither has a commercial integration with the other. If the session completes and settles correctly, the interoperability model works.

Step 1 of 11

  1. Charger connects

    Charger A comes online and registers with CPO A's platform using OCPP.

  2. Status and metering flow

    Availability, connector state and meter values are reported continuously over the OCPP link.

  3. CPO publishes over OCPI

    CPO A exposes locations, EVSEs, connectors and tariffs through the interoperability gateway.

  4. eMSP discovers the charger

    eMSP B, operating an entirely separate platform, retrieves Charger A's location and tariff. No bilateral integration was built.

  5. Driver selects the charger

    A customer of eMSP B sees a charger belonging to CPO A in their own application.

  6. Authorised start

    The start request is authorised by eMSP B, carried over OCPI and executed by CPO A on the charger.

  7. Session created and exchanged

    Session data updates in both operators' systems while the vehicle charges.

  8. CDR generated

    The session ends and CPO A issues a Charge Detail Record to eMSP B in a standard format.

  9. Payment and reconciliation recorded

    Settlement data between the two operators is represented and reconciled. Commercial terms remain a matter for the parties.

  10. National platform receives monitoring data

    Authorised, standardised infrastructure and status data reaches the government layer. Customer identity does not.

  11. Dashboard reflects the estate

    Charger, location, operator, status, session and compliance state are visible to government in one place.

Interoperability is proved by heterogeneity, not by volume. Two operators who have never integrated with each other demonstrate more than twenty who share a vendor. Government does not need to onboard the industry to begin.

Indicative delivery programme

Twelve to sixteen weeks from definition to evaluation

Phases overlap deliberately. Integration with participant systems begins while the platform is still being built, because integration is where the real findings emerge.

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Programme definition

    Confirm pilot scope, participants, technical requirements and governance. Define the test cases against which everything will later be judged.

  2. Weeks 2–4

    Architecture and design

    Technical architecture, data model, API specifications, registry design and dashboard design.

  3. Weeks 4–8

    Platform development

    Registration, registry, gateway, APIs, authentication and dashboard.

  4. Weeks 6–10

    Interoperability integration

    OCPP testing, OCPI integration, and connection of participating CPO, eMSP and charger systems.

  5. Weeks 9–12

    Testing

    Functional, security, interoperability, CDR and failure testing.

  6. Weeks 12–14

    Controlled pilot

    Participant onboarding, live test scenarios, certification tests and monitoring.

  7. Weeks 14–16

    Evaluation

    Pilot report, gap analysis, standards recommendations, national-scale roadmap and procurement recommendations.

Where the pilot sits in the five-year roadmap

The four-phase national roadmap set out earlier in this proposal is unchanged. Phase 1 remains Foundation at 0–12 months, Phase 2 the national pilot at 12–24 months, Phase 3 national scale at 24–36 months, and Phase 4 advanced electric mobility across three to five years.

The Lean MVP is an accelerated technical demonstration inside the early Foundation and Pilot stages. It does not extend the roadmap, and it does not sit outside it. It brings forward the evidence on which Phase 2 and Phase 3 decisions will depend.

Evaluation

What the Lean MVP must prove

Six questions, each answerable from the pilot itself rather than from projection or precedent elsewhere.

Technical

Can different charger and software systems communicate?

Operational

Can charging sessions move correctly across systems?

Commercial

Can different operators support interoperable charging without giving up control of their businesses?

Regulatory

Can government monitor compliance without becoming the operator?

Data

Can government obtain a reliable national-level view of infrastructure?

Investment

Can an open technical framework reduce fragmentation and make the Nigerian market easier to invest in?

How results are recorded

The pilot deliberately avoids numerical performance targets, which would have to be invented before any baseline exists. Results are recorded qualitatively against defined workflows.

Pass

A participating system successfully completes the required interoperability workflow.

Fail

A required interoperability workflow cannot be completed.

Conditional

The system works but requires remediation.

Certified

The participant satisfies the defined pilot requirements.

Strategic value to government

The pilot is not only a technical exercise. It changes the basis on which the national programme is decided, funded and procured.

Lower initial risk

Government validates the model before committing to national deployment.

Lower initial cost

Only the minimum technical infrastructure is built in the first instance.

Faster demonstration

Interoperability can be shown within months, rather than waiting for a full national programme to complete.

Industry participation

Private-sector operators help shape the national standard rather than receiving it.

Evidence-based policy

Decisions rest on actual interoperability testing against real hardware and real platforms.

Investment confidence

A functioning interoperability framework signals that Nigeria is building an open market, not fragmented technology islands.

  • CPO platforms
  • eMSPs
  • Payment services
  • Energy systems
  • Fleet services
  • Consumer applications

Government should establish the rules and standards; the private sector should build and operate the market.

The pilot must not create a private national platform

The Lean MVP is government-led. It may be privately developed or procured, and it must remain open to multiple technology providers. Specifications, interfaces and test criteria produced during the pilot should be published so that any qualified supplier can build against them.

Nothing in this section should be read as proposing an exclusive vendor, proprietary national technology, a monopoly position, or a single CPO, eMSP or hardware manufacturer. A pilot that produced any of those outcomes would have failed on its own terms, because the objective is an open national framework.

A signal to the international market

A working interoperability pilot is evidence that can be shown to international charging companies, vehicle and charger manufacturers, energy companies, infrastructure investors, technology companies and financial institutions.

For each of them, the question about an emerging EV market is the same: will the systems we deploy here still work in three years, or will we be asked to rebuild them for every network we join?

Nigeria is establishing the rules and technical architecture that allow multiple companies to participate without rebuilding incompatible systems.

No commitments, partnerships, letters of intent or expressions of interest from any company, investor or institution are claimed or implied anywhere in this section.

Recommended starting point

The Federal Government does not need to implement the entire national framework simultaneously. The recommended sequence is deliberately short.

  1. Establish the policy.
  2. Build a controlled interoperability sandbox.
  3. Connect a small number of real participants.
  4. Demonstrate OCPP and OCPI interoperability.
  5. Test monitoring and certification.
  6. Evaluate the results.
  7. Then scale nationally.
Prove the interoperability model first. Scale the national infrastructure second.

The long-term objective set out throughout this proposal is unchanged: an open, connected, safe, transparent and investment-ready electric mobility ecosystem for Nigeria, built on common standards, a national registry, certification and monitoring, and extending in time to ISO 15118, Plug & Charge, smart charging, vehicle-to-grid and cross-border African interoperability.

The Lean MVP is not the destination. It is the fastest credible path toward a national interoperable electric mobility ecosystem.
